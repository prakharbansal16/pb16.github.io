Cosmological Tensions

Background

Cosmology is in an unusual situation: our standard model, $\Lambda$CDM, fits the data remarkably well — and yet, as measurements have become more precise, cracks have started to appear. These "tensions" are disagreements between independent measurements of the same quantity that are too large to be explained by random statistical fluctuations alone.

The most famous is the Hubble tension. The Hubble constant $H_0$ tells us how fast the universe is expanding today. We can infer it from the very early universe (using the Cosmic Microwave Background, or CMB) or measure it directly in the local universe using a chain of distance indicators — Cepheid variable stars, Type Ia supernovae, and others. The problem is that these two methods give values of $H_0$ that are now discrepant at the $\sim 5\text{–}7\sigma$ level. As both measurements have become more precise over the past decade, the tension has only grown more stubborn, ruling out simple statistical flukes.

A newer and arguably more dramatic tension involves dark energy. In the standard $\Lambda$CDM model, dark energy is a cosmological constant $\Lambda$ — its energy density does not change with time, and it drives the accelerating expansion of the universe. But data from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), combined with CMB and Type Ia supernova measurements, have shown growing evidence that this may not be the case. The data hint at dark energy that evolves with time — a so-called dynamical dark energy (DDE) model. If this holds up, it would be one of the most significant discoveries in physics since the original detection of cosmic acceleration in 1998.

My Work

The key question I worked on is: what specific features in the data are driving this preference for dynamical dark energy? Rather than simply fitting a DDE model and quoting a significance, we tried to isolate which redshift range and which dataset combinations are responsible for the signal.

Working with my advisor Dragan Huterer, I adopted a flexible parameterisation of the expansion history — allowing the expansion rate $H(z)$ to vary freely in redshift bins, rather than assuming a specific dark energy equation of state. This makes it possible to "read off" where the data deviates from $\Lambda$CDM, without pre-assuming the shape of the deviation. We found a consistent preference across datasets for a 3–4% increase in the expansion rate at $z \approx 0.7$ relative to the $\Lambda$CDM prediction. This is the redshift where DESI's LRG (Luminous Red Galaxy) sample has the most constraining power, and it is the primary driver of the DDE preference. A key part of this work was developing a simple but accurate compression of the CMB likelihood that allowed us to efficiently combine CMB information with DESI BAO data (see also the CMB Compression page).
